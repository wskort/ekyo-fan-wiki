# Measured Flame
## Chapter I — The Edge of the Room

The drawing room had been arranged to appear effortless. Clara Ashbourne suspected the effort had been considerable. Late afternoon light entered through three tall windows and stretched in pale divisions across the floor, catching at the gilt edges of chairs before thinning into shadow. The fire had been laid but not yet stirred; its quiet promise rested in the grate. A pianoforte stood open near a cluster of young ladies who turned pages of music with animation that had not yet resolved into performance.

Clara sat near the middle of the three windows, where the light reached her shoulder but did not fall directly upon her face. The chair was narrow but well upholstered; she had chosen it because it allowed her a view of both the hearth and the doorway without placing her in either path — near enough to be included, far enough to escape notice.

Her gloves lay folded upon the small table at her elbow. Her hands rested one over the other in her lap, still except for the faint adjustment of a thumb against knuckle. She let the room assemble itself before her, as it so often did.

Near the centre stood the illustrious Miss Vale.

Light settled easily upon Celestine’s shoulders and along the smooth line of her bodice. A gentleman faced her at comfortable proximity, his stance easy without presumption. His coat was cut with understated precision; his boots bore the faint polish of recent attention. Straight blond hair, arranged not in the common swept-back style but slightly to one side, lay across his forehead in a deliberate fall that shifted when he inclined his head.

“You give me too much credit, Miss Vale,” he was saying, amusement resting lightly at the edge of his voice. “I assure you, my judgment improves only in proportion to my company.”

“And deteriorates when it is less select?” Celestine replied.

“I should never confess so bold a truth.”

“Of course not, sir. Confession would require sincerity.” Her friendly tone did little to remove the sharpness of her judgement. 

His mouth curved. “Then I am in danger of being misunderstood.”

Celestine did not laugh. A faint alteration touched her expression — as though his remark had nearly succeeded. 

Across the room, Mrs. Denham’s voice rose in brisk consultation with a cousin near the hearth. One of the young ladies at the pianoforte struck a tentative chord and immediately withdrew her hand, apologising to no one in particular.

The gentleman’s gaze shifted as though in idle survey, passing the pianoforte, the hearth, the third window—

—and pausing, just briefly, where Clara sat in the diluted light.

Clara stilled. She resented the warmth rising beneath her collar. 

The fire in the grate clicked faintly as a log settled.

When Celestine spoke again, her tone had altered by no more than a shade.

“You will find Hartwell Grove instructive, I think,” she said calmly. “One is obliged to observe carefully here.”

“I pride myself on that very skill.”

“Do you?” Clara couldn't see her eyes from here, but she knew Celestine's dark blue eyes would be piercing. The pause that followed was not long, but it was sufficient.

His expression sharpened. “I should be mortified,” he said, “to discover that my talents have been overrated within the first quarter hour of our acquaintance.”

“Mortification implies ambition,” Celestine replied. “I have not yet determined whether you possess any.”

He inclined his head, accepting the correction as though it were a compliment. The fall of fair hair shifted slightly across his brow.

“Then I must endeavour to display some.”

“Endeavour quietly,” she said. “Hartwell Grove rewards restraint.”

“Restraint,” he repeated. “That is a quality seldom admired.”

“It is seldom practised.”

At the pianoforte, the country air resumed, more confident now. A pair of gentlemen drew nearer to listen, their voices lowered in deference to the attempt at harmony.

“And what, precisely,” he asked, “does restraint consist of in such a room as this?”

“In observing without announcing it,” Celestine replied. 

“And you suppose that possible?”

“I know it to be.” There was a hint of amusement in her voice.

He smiled at that — not broadly, but with interest.

“Then I must be careful.”

“Careful?” she echoed.

“Not to be observed in the act of observing.”

“Endeavour,” she said evenly, “as much as one is able.”

The remark settled between them.

He did not answer at once. Instead, his gaze shifted again — not hurriedly, not in the restless manner of a man seeking diversion, but with an exactness that suggested decision rather than curiosity. It passed the hearth. It passed the cluster at the pianoforte. It moved toward the windows.

Clara felt the alteration before she named it. The sense of being included in a line of sight that had, until then, travelled elsewhere. She looked at her folded hands. An unwelcome quickening of breath overcame her. 

When she looked up again, his attention did not move away at once. There was no smile in it now. Only assessment. 

The music faltered on a missed note and resumed. Mrs. Denham laughed too brightly at something her cousin had said. The late light along the floor had thinned to a cooler shade.

“I see,” he said softly, returning his gaze to Celestine. “Observation is rarely so discreet as one intends.”

Celestine regarded him without turning her head.

“That depends,” she said, “upon what one intends.” The faintest suggestion of a smile rested at the edge of her mouth — not invitation, not dismissal.

Across the room, Mrs. Denham began inquiring whether the gentlemen preferred claret before dinner. A servant entered and withdrew again. The music at the pianoforte came to an uncertain conclusion and dissolved into light conversation.

The gentleman inclined his head, as though acknowledging a move well made. “And what,” he said, “do you suppose I intend, Miss Vale?”

Celestine did not answer at once. Her gaze moved — not hurriedly, not evasively — but outward, as though considering the room itself as part of her reply. It passed the hearth. The instrument. The doorway. The windows. When her eyes met Clara's, she did not pause long enough to be remarked upon, but Clara felt herself shrink a little as though the room had briefly realigned. 

Celestine returned her attention to the gentleman.

“Intention,” she said at last, “is best proven in conduct.”

“Then I shall endeavour to conduct myself impeccably.”

“I recommend it.” 

She shifted then, not away from him, but slightly aside — just enough to allow another gentleman, who had hovered within reach, to step forward.

“Mr. Fletcher,” she said, with effortless courtesy, “you were describing the riding paths.”

Mr. Fletcher, gratified, resumed his account at once. Celestine listened with attention perfectly proportioned to the subject.

The blond gentleman stood momentarily displaced. Not dismissed. Repositioned.

Celestine allowed Mr. Fletcher precisely three sentences. Then she said, without raising her voice, “Mr. Harrow.”

The name settled into the room with quiet clarity. He turned immediately.

“There is someone,” she said, “to whom you have not yet been introduced.” Her tone was neither pointed nor playful. It carried no more weight than necessary.

He glanced involuntarily at Clara. For a fraction of a second, no more, something like satisfaction crossed his face, as though a suspicion had been confirmed. “I stand reproved,” he said lightly.

“I should regret to see you remiss on your first afternoon.”

“I shall endeavour to repair the omission.”

She inclined her head. And then, with the ease of someone who had concluded a matter, she returned her attention to Mr. Fletcher’s riding paths as though no adjustment had been made at all.

Mr. Harrow did not hesitate. He crossed the room without haste, though the path he chose was direct.

Clara felt her shoulders tense as she saw him coming. She rose before he reached her.

He stopped at a proper distance and bowed.

“Miss Ashbourne,” he said, “I must beg your pardon. It appears I have been deficient in my introductions.”

Clara inclined her head in return. “Then I am indebted to Miss Vale for your correction, sir.”

A faint smile touched his mouth. “Correction implies prior fault.”

“Does it not?”

His eyes held hers a moment longer than strict necessity required. “Then I shall hope,” he said, “to prove myself improved.”

Clara resumed her seat, and he did the same, selecting a chair angled slightly toward hers without presuming intimacy. The space between them remained correct — neither crowded nor distant.

“I fear,” he continued, “that I have already betrayed my character.”

“So soon?” she asked.

“I was informed,” he said, lowering his voice only enough to distinguish their conversation from the rest of the room, “that Hartwell Grove rewards restraint. I find I have begun poorly.”

“In what respect?”

“I have been insufficiently reserved.”

Clara regarded him for a moment. “Indeed? I had not observed you to be otherwise.”

“Then I am doubly exposed.”

“How unfortunate.”

The faintest laugh escaped him — genuine this time, unpolished by performance. “I begin to suspect,” he said, “that you are less easily impressed than most of our company.”

“That would imply I have been offered an occasion for it.”

“Have you not?” he asked, "Surely you attend more assemblies such as this."

She did not answer immediately. The light along the wall had cooled; the fire, still unstirred, gave off only the faint scent of wood and ash. The log split with a sudden crack. “You mistake,” she said at last, “attendance for engagement.”

“And you make a distinction between the two?”

“Certainly.”

“And which,” he asked, “do you favour?”

“Engagement requires notice.”

“Then I shall attempt it.”

She hesitated. “Restraint,” she said, “was recommended.”

“I am attempting restraint,” he replied. “You see how it costs me.”

She did not, but she offered, “Very dearly, I imagine.”

He studied her then, not with the open admiration of a man determined to flatter, but with an interest that had sharpened since crossing the room. “You were observing us,” he said. The statement was not accusatory.

Clara felt her cheeks warming. She lowered her gaze deliberately. 

“One cannot avoid observing what is placed directly before one.”

“And was I placed?”

“You were conspicuous," she admitted.

“In what way?”

You were displayed, she thought. “You were speaking," she said.

A quiet laugh escaped him. “That is a vice I must endeavour to correct," he said.

“You consider speaking a vice, Mr. Harrow?”

“I have been advised that intention must be proven in conduct.”

“And how do you consider your conduct?” she asked, trying to keep her tone neutral.

He leaned in slightly. “I consider it a beginning.”

The music at the pianoforte dissolved again into scattered chords. Mrs. Denham called for someone to fetch candles; the light had grown insufficient for embroidery, she declared.

He did not look away from Clara. “And you, Miss Ashbourne,” he said, “do you favour beginnings?”

She met his gaze, but she could detect no mockery in his attention. “I favour conclusions. They make no promises.” 

Something flickered in his expression — not offence, not amusement, but recognition.

“Then I must be careful not to disappoint you.”

“You must be careful not to presume.” The syllables left her mouth too cleanly cut. She flinched a little at her own tone. 

The faintest pause.

He inclined his head, accepting the correction.

“I see,” he said quietly, “that I have been set a more difficult task than I anticipated.”

Her eyes narrowed slightly. “What task?” Clara asked.

He did not hesitate. “Merely one of circumstance.”

“Circumstance is rarely deliberate.”

“No,” he agreed. “But it may be useful.”

She regarded him, careful not to alter her straight posture. Around them, the drawing room had begun to shift toward the practicalities of evening. A servant entered with a taper and moved toward the sconces; candlelight caught along the gilt frames and altered the proportions of the room. Shadows lengthened where daylight had thinned.

“And what use,” she asked, “do you propose to make of it?”

“I am not yet certain.”

“That seems an imprudent admission.”

“On the contrary,” he said. “Certainty, when premature, is more dangerous.” His eyes twinkled in amusement. 

“You speak as though you have had experience of it.”

“I have had experience of impatience.”

“And which proved the greater fault?”

He allowed himself a breath that was almost a laugh. “That remains under investigation.”

Something in Clara sharpened, her attention engaged rather than withheld. “You appear,” she said, “to enjoy inquiry.”

“I do.”

“And the object?”

“Is rarely as instructive as one hopes.”

“Then why persist?”

“Because occasionally,” he said, and here his voice lowered by a degree that did not quite breach propriety, “one encounters something less predictable.”

The words hung between them. She could have dismissed the remark. It invited dismissal. It offered no explicit claim, no impropriety upon which to take offence. Instead, she said, “Predictability is often a courtesy.”

“To whom?”

“To those who prefer not to be surprised.”

“And you, Miss Ashbourne?”

“I prefer accuracy.”

“In others?”

“In myself.”

For a moment, the room receded. Not in sound — the pianoforte had resumed, someone laughed near the hearth — but in relevance.

He studied her with an expression no longer amused.

“And are you accurate,” he asked, “in your assessment of me?”

“You are attentive,” she said. “And you dislike being disregarded.”

A flicker — swift, involuntary — crossed his face. “Disregarded?” he repeated.

“You crossed the room.”

“Under instruction.”

“You might have declined.”

“And incur Miss Vale’s disapproval?”

“That has not appeared to trouble you.”

He smiled again, but it no longer carried the ease of earlier moments. “You attribute too much calculation to me.”

“And too little?” she ventured softly.

The question landed cleanly. He did not answer at once.

A servant announced that dinner would be served in ten minutes. Chairs shifted. Conversations adjusted themselves toward conclusion.

“You are mistaken in one respect,” he said at last.

“And that is?”

“I do not dislike being disregarded.”

“No?”

“I dislike being unconsidered.” The distinction lingered. The precision of it did not escape her.

Clara rose as others began to do the same. “So long as you are observed,” she said evenly, “you need not fear it.”

He stood as well. “And you, Miss Ashbourne?”

“Yes?”

“Do you object to being observed?” 

Clara considered herself as she must appear to him. A quiet girl, sheathed in simple linen, her hair unadorned and her posture unassuming. Content to remain in the periphery of others. The light from the nearest candle touched her cheek; the rest of her remained in the softer shade of the window’s fading day. “Only,” she said softly, “to being misunderstood.”

She inclined her head.

He bowed.

Neither offered more. The current between them remained — contained, unspent, unresolved.

## Chapter II — A Measured Spark

The door to the dining room stood open long enough to release a gradual procession of guests into the corridor. Voices carried more freely now, softened by wine and warmth. Laughter rose and dissolved without consequence.

Clara walked beside Mrs. Denham’s cousin, who spoke at length about the superiority of Hertfordshire peaches to any grown further north. Clara assented at appropriate intervals. The candles along the corridor had been lit; their flames leaned slightly in the draught as doors opened and closed.

When they re-entered the drawing room, it no longer resembled the measured chamber of the afternoon.

The fire had been encouraged into flame. Its light trembled along the lower panels of the walls and threw movement into the gilt frames. Candles stood in clusters upon the mantel and along the side tables, so that the room glowed rather than shone. Shadows no longer withdrew politely to the corners; they gathered where they pleased.

The chairs had shifted during dinner. They never resumed their original positions.

Clara paused just within the threshold, allowing others to pass before her. The heat from the hearth reached further now. The scent of woodsmoke mingled faintly with wax and perfume. She did not immediately look toward the centre of the room.

Mrs. Denham’s cousin was claimed by a gentleman eager to continue the debate on fruit. Clara stepped aside and reclaimed a seat not far from where she had sat before — not precisely the same, but near enough to afford her a view of the hearth and the far wall.

There was a piece of lint on her glove. She removed it. 

The conversation around her reassembled itself in looser clusters. Two of the young ladies approached the pianoforte with greater confidence than before; the instrument answered more decisively under candlelight.

Only then did she allow her gaze to move.

He stood nearer the fire this time, one hand resting lightly upon the back of a chair, speaking to a pair of gentlemen with an ease that suggested he had not been idle at dinner. The flame caught in his hair and lent it a brighter hue. When he inclined his head to listen, the fall across his brow shifted as it had earlier.

He laughed — briefly — and something in the sound was sharper than before. As though he felt it, he turned.

The room did not still. No one paused in their conversation. Yet the moment extended itself with quiet clarity.

His gaze found her without wandering first. Not by accident.

Clara did not look away.

The fire snapped softly in the grate.

He did not bow across the room, nor incline his head in exaggerated recognition. Instead, after the barest interval, he resumed his conversation — but not entirely.

A few minutes passed.

A gentleman drew him further toward the mantel. Mrs. Denham called for additional candles near the pianoforte. One of the younger guests proposed a song, which was declined with polite reluctance.

Clara remained seated. She told herself she had not been waiting.

When he disengaged, it was without announcement. He excused himself with a murmur that did not carry and crossed the carpet with a stride neither hurried nor slow. No one intervened. No one directed him. He stopped before her.

“Miss Ashbourne,” he said, with a formality that might have belonged to a first introduction rather than a second meeting, “I trust dinner has not diminished your opinion of the company.”

“I should require evidence before forming one,” she replied.

“Then I must supply it.”

“Must you?”

“I find myself compelled.”

She regarded him steadily.

“Compulsion,” she said, “is rarely elegant.”

“Then I shall endeavour to disguise it.”

A faint smile threatened at the corner of her mouth but did not settle. “Pray do,” she said.

He did not take the chair immediately beside her. Instead, he selected one opposite, forcing their conversation into alignment rather than intimacy. “I had hoped,” he said, “that we might resume. You declared a preference for conclusions.”

“And you objected.”

“I did not object.”

“You implied dissatisfaction.”

“I implied curiosity.” She regarded him evenly. “And you expect it satisfied this evening?”

“I expect nothing,” he said. “I prefer to discover.”

“That is convenient.”

“For whom?”

“For the discoverer.”

The fire shifted in the grate, sending a brief pulse of warmth between them.

Across the room, Miss Vale stood near the pianoforte, speaking to Mrs. Denham with easy composure. She did not look in their direction.

“You favour conclusions,” Mr Harrow said quietly. “What occupies the interval?”

Clara did not answer at once. “The interval,” she said at last, “is often the only portion worth attending to.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one you are likely to receive.”

He did not smile. “Very well,” he said. “Then tell me this instead — what occupies you when you are not enduring rooms?”

“I do not observe rooms,” Clara replied. “I endure them.”

“That is a harsher verdict than Hartwell Grove deserves.”

“Is it?”

“I had hoped you might find it diverting.”

“Diversion is not occupation.”

“Then what is?”

She did not hesitate this time. “Reading.” The word did not fall lightly.

“Novels?” he asked.

“Among other things.”

“And what do you seek in them?”

She considered him briefly, as though weighing whether the question merited sincerity.

“Intensity,” she said.

The answer seemed to surprise him. “In what form?”

“Consequence.”

He studied her now with something more focused than amusement. “Explain.”

“In most rooms,” she said, her tone still measured but no longer detached, “one speaks and nothing alters. One enters, one leaves, and the furniture remains precisely where it was.”

“And in novels?”

“One speaks,” she said, “and something happens.” The faintest colour had risen to her cheek. Not embarrassment. Animation.

“At least,” she continued, “something ought to.”

“And when it does not?”

“Then it has failed.” There was no irony in her expression now. No defensive civility.

He watched her as though she had shifted without moving. “And do you prefer the tragic sort of consequence,” he asked quietly, “or the triumphant?”

“I prefer the honest.”

“Which is?”

“Where the feeling matches the act.”

The music at the pianoforte altered key, but neither of them turned toward it.

“And do you read aloud?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“To whom?”

“Mrs. Vale.”

“And do you inhabit the text,” he asked, “or merely pronounce it?” The question landed closer than he perhaps intended.

She held his gaze. “One does not merely pronounce it,” she said. “If one is to read properly.”

“Properly?”

“One must allow it.”

“Allow what?”

“Everything.” The word left her more forcefully than the others.

A beat passed.

Her shoulders drew back. The colour in her cheek cooled. “I mean,” she said evenly, “one must attend to it.”

But he had heard the difference. “You do not sound as though you merely attend,” he said.

“That is fortunate.”

He did not laugh. “And you would not read for anyone else?”

“I have not been invited.”

“That can be remedied.”

She met that directly. “You presume invitation.”

“I express interest.”

“Interest,” she said, “is easily withdrawn.”

“Not always.”

A quiet challenge passed between them — not flirtation now, but recognition.

“You crossed the room,” she said.

“Yes.”

“That suggests intention.”

“It does,” he admitted.

“And?”

“And I wished to see whether you would speak as you have just done.”

Clara felt her chest tightening. “And now that you have?” she asked, trying to sound unaffected.

He did not answer at once. “I am less inclined,” he said finally, “to be easily satisfied.” The remark was softer than the earlier ones. Not boastful. Not teasing. Simply true.

Across the room, chairs began to move. A gentleman declared that the evening required movement. Laughter followed.

He rose. “Perhaps,” he said, extending no hand yet making the intention clear, “we might test the matter.”

“Only one set,” Mrs. Denham declared. “We shall not exhaust ourselves.”

Chairs were drawn back with more enthusiasm than care. A clear space opened at the centre of the room. Candles were shifted nearer; the fire answered with a low, steady glow.

Miss Vale stepped forward. “If we are determined to attempt it,” she said, “we may as well do so properly.” 

There was no objection.

She took her place near the edge of the cleared floor and named the set — not loudly, but with a tone that admitted no confusion. Partners arranged themselves without hesitation.

Clara did not look toward her.

“Miss Ashbourne,” he said beside her, as though the matter had only just occurred to him, “will you stand up with me?” He did not extend his hand immediately. He waited. 

The room had shifted into expectation. Refusal would have been conspicuous.

She rose. “You assume I am unengaged,” she said.

“I observed no claim.”

“Observation again.”

“It serves.”

She placed her hand in his.

His fingers closed with an assurance that suggested he had not doubted her acceptance.

They took their places in the line.

Across the floor, Miss Vale called the first figure. Her voice carried cleanly through the room, shaping movement without strain.

The music began.

They advanced and retreated as prescribed. Distance enforced itself before intimacy could. Clara was conscious first of the pattern — the geometry of it — rather than of him. 

Until their hands met. A brief turn. Palm to palm. Correct pressure. Correct duration. Nothing in it transgressed. And yet the contact did not feel incidental.

They separated.

“You dance precisely,” he said.

“It is required.”

“Precision can conceal a great deal.”

“Such as?” she asked.

“Intent.”

The next figure drew them closer again. They passed shoulder to shoulder before turning outward. His coat brushed the sleeve of her gown. The contact was fleeting and accidental in appearance.

“You attribute much to choreography,” she said.

“Do you not?”

“I attribute it to design.”

“And the designer?”

She did not answer.

Miss Vale called the next figure.

They turned, crossed, reformed.

This time when he took her hand to lead her down the line, the hold was firmer — not improper, but deliberate.

“You are not easily hurried,” he said quietly.

“I do not run.”

“Even when pursued?”

“That depends on the pursuer.”

The corner of his mouth shifted. “And what would recommend him?”

“Discretion.”

“That is a discouraging criterion.”

“It should be.”

They completed the figure and faced one another again. The fire’s warmth reached them more fully at this angle; candlelight caught in the pale strands of his hair.

“You mistake me,” he said.

“In what respect?”

“I do not mistake resistance for refusal.”

The music swept them apart before she could reply.

When they returned to face each other at the close of the set, she said only, “You mistake very little, I think.”

The final notes dissolved into modest applause.

He did not release her hand at once. Only when the line broke did he step back.

“We are released,” she said.

“For now,” he replied.

The room dissolved back into conversation. Chairs were reclaimed. Laughter rose in uneven pockets. One of the younger gentlemen attempted to reconstruct a missed step for the amusement of his partner and failed with enthusiasm.

Clara withdrew her hand. The absence of his touch registered more distinctly than the contact had.

“You are consistent,” he said, not loudly enough to invite attention.

“In what regard?”

“You do not answer directly.”

“I have answered you.”

“Selectively.”

“That is the only safe method.”

“Safe,” he repeated, as though weighing the word. “You do not appear particularly concerned with safety.”

“That depends on the hazard.”

“And am I one?” The question did not carry flirtation now. It was asked plainly.

“You are a guest,” she said.

“That was not the question.”

She did not move away. The space between them remained narrow, though no longer structured by music. “You are attentive,” she said at last. “That can be inconvenient.”

“Do you object?”

Her gaze held his without softening. “That would be wise.”

“Why?”

“Because attention rarely diminishes.”

Something like approval — or recognition — flickered across his expression. “I do not diminish easily,” he said.

“No,” she replied. “You do not.”

Around them, conversation thickened. Mrs. Denham called for refreshments. A servant passed close enough to force a slight adjustment in their stance, but neither withdrew entirely.

“Will you read tomorrow?” he asked, as though the thought had occurred to him without premeditation.

“If required.”

“I require it.”

“You mistake your authority.”

“And if I were to ask?”

“You have.”

“And?”

“And I have not answered.”

He watched her for a long moment — not amused, not testing now. Assessing. “I shall hear you,” he said quietly, “whether you intend it or not.” The words were not a threat. They were a promise of attention.

She felt the heat rise again — not from the fire, but from somewhere less contained. “You presume much,” she said.

“I observe.”

Across the room, Miss Vale accepted a glass of wine from Mrs. Denham. For a fleeting instant, her gaze passed over the cluster near the hearth. It did not linger.

Harrow stepped back at last. “I shall not claim another set,” he said. “You have already declared yourself sufficient for the evening.”

“That was not what I declared.”

A faint smile returned — restrained, but unmistakable. “Then I am relieved.”

He bowed , not deeply, but enough to signal departure. He did not seek another partner.

Clara remained where she was.

The fire settled lower in the grate. Candlelight trembled along the floorboards.

For the first time since dinner, she felt not observed — but anticipated.

## Chapter III — Morning Air

Morning entered Hartwell Grove without ceremony. Light pressed insistently at the curtains long before the bell was rung. Clara had been awake before it sounded, aware of the ceiling above her and the precise recollection of a hand closing over hers in the turn of the dance. She lay still until the house began to stir.

By the time she descended, the brightness had settled into the corridors with an authority that candlelight had never claimed. Nothing blurred in daylight. Nothing softened. 

The breakfast room was already animated. Silver covers lifted and replaced; porcelain chimed faintly; Mrs. Denham praised the preserves with cheerful insistence. Conversation flowed easily in several directions at once.

Clara took the seat she had occupied the previous morning. It was not assigned, but it had become hers by repetition. She unfolded her napkin with care. She poured tea without spilling. She did not look toward the far end of the table.

She was conscious of where he sat without turning her head. His voice carried more distinctly in the morning — less burnished, more exact. He spoke of the grounds, of the clarity of the weather, of the advantage of taking air before noon. Others answered him readily.

She buttered her bread with more attention than the task required. If she did not encourage recollection, the previous evening might remain contained within it — a matter of atmosphere, of music, of misinterpreted proximity.

Celestine sat several places away, composed as ever, listening to Mrs. Denham with an expression of polite engagement. Clara did not look at her either. It would be foolish to behave differently.

When the suggestion was made to take a turn along the south path before the sun grew oppressive, it was received with approval. Chairs shifted. Shawls were claimed. Parasols opened with soft, decisive snaps.

Clara rose quickly and attached herself to Mrs. Denham’s cousin and two of the younger ladies, who were debating the superiority of roses to lilies in early summer borders. It was a harmless subject. It required no self-exposure. They passed through the French doors into brightness.

The garden lay in disciplined arrangement — gravel paths cut with precision between clipped hedges, beds already inclining toward excess, the distant line of trees marking the boundary of the property. Sunlight struck everything with unforgiving clarity.

Clara adjusted her glove. She would not look behind her.

Footsteps sounded upon the gravel — multiple, indistinguishable. Laughter rose somewhere to her left. A gentleman called out that the orchard might yield an early pear.

The group rearranged itself with the unstudied elasticity of morning company. One of the younger ladies darted ahead to inspect a bloom too large for its stem. Mrs. Denham’s cousin slowed to comment upon the symmetry of the hedges.

Clara matched her pace to theirs deliberately. If she remained within a cluster, there would be no opportunity for misinterpretation.

The path narrowed. She became aware, without hearing him speak, that Mr Harrow had drawn nearer. Her breath altered before she could steady it. 
 
Without any visible manoeuvre, he was beside her. Not abrupt. Not intrusive. Simply there. “Miss Ashbourne,” he said, greeting her for the first time that day, “I trust the morning does not offend you.”

She did not start. She would not give him that satisfaction. “It has not yet made the attempt,” she replied.

“Then it is fortunate.”

“For whom?”

“For those who must walk within it.”

She kept her gaze fixed upon the path ahead. “You are in high spirits, Mr. Harrow.”

“It is a bright morning. You cannot disapprove of brightness?”

“I do not.”

He fell silent for a step or two. Gravel shifted beneath their feet in quiet rhythm. “I had thought,” he said at last, “that candlelight flattered more generously.”

Her pulse quickened despite herself. “It conceals.”

“And daylight reveals?”

“If one is attentive.” She wished she had chosen a less charged word.

“And are you attentive this morning?” She could feel his attention without turning toward him.

“I am deliberate,” she said.

“Only when necessary?”

She turned her head then — not fully, only enough to acknowledge the echo. “Precisely.”

For a moment neither looked away.

They walked in parallel along the narrowing path. Gravel shifted beneath their steps in an even rhythm. The roses trained along the trellis were already too open for her liking; their colour felt excessive in this light. She fixed her attention upon their arrangement — the clean discipline of stems forced where they did not naturally grow.

“You prefer daylight,” he said after a few steps.

She had known he would not leave her to silence. “I prefer clarity.” It was safer than warmth. Safer than the memory of his hand closing over hers in the turn.

“That is not always the same thing.”

“No.” She kept her gaze ahead. The others had drawn slightly forward; their voices blurred into an indistinct thread. A pair behind them lingered over a broken stem. If she slowed, she might rejoin them. If she quickened, she would seem hurried.

“You are different this morning,” he said. The remark struck closer than she wished.

“The light is different,” she replied. That was true. Under candlelight, the room had dissolved at the edges. It had been possible to forget the geometry of it.

“That is not what I meant.”

“I know.” She did not want him to see that she knew.

“You withdraw,” he said.

She felt the word settle somewhere uncomfortably accurate. “I proceed with care.” Care was respectable. Care was defensible. Care could not be censured by anyone — least of all Celestine.

“From what?”

From herself, she thought. From the ease with which she had said _everything_. She let the question rest between them long enough to feel deliberate. “From excess,” she said.

The hedge opened briefly before narrowing again between disciplined lines of green. Sunlight struck the gravel hard enough to make her narrow her eyes. It left no forgiving shadows.

“You would prefer,” he said, “that last night be attributed to arrangement.”

The music. The figures. Miss Vale’s voice calling the turns.

“It was arranged.”

“The figures were.”

Her pulse quickened without permission. She remembered the firmness of his hand at the close of the set. The way he had not released her at once. She did not answer.

A younger lady laughed ahead of them, bending too close to a bloom and staining her glove with pollen. The sound carried and faded.

“I do not mistake atmosphere for invention,” he said. Atmosphere. As though the room had manufactured it.

“I did not suggest invention.”

“No.” The word fell cleanly. She could not tell whether it absolved or accused.

The breeze lifted the edge of her ribbon and let it fall again. She resisted the urge to adjust it; the gesture would betray distraction. “Candlelight makes boldness seem harmless,” she said, as though remarking upon the garden. She had not intended to speak that aloud.

“And daylight renders it so?”

“No. Daylight remembers.” The admission unsettled her the moment it left her mouth. Daylight remembered — and so did she.

The path divided ahead. Orchard to the right, enclosed garden to the left. Mrs. Denham’s voice floated from the leftward shade, calling those who preferred relief from the sun.

He did not move immediately. “You regulate,” he said.

She inclined her head once. Regulation was habit. Regulation had been expected of her since she entered the Vale household. Gratitude in posture, composure in tone, no excess of any kind.

“And you?” she asked before she could stop herself. “Do you?” Why had she asked that? It invited disclosure.

He looked at her then, without amusement. The absence of levity unsettled her more than teasing would have done. “I seldom retreat,” he said. 

The word struck her more sharply than she cared to admit. Clara turned toward the shaded path.

The air shifted almost immediately — cooler beneath the low trees, the light broken into fragments along the gravel. The noise of the larger party thinned behind them; the enclosed garden absorbed sound differently, as though reluctant to carry it.

He followed without comment. They walked a few steps in quiet. She was acutely aware of the space between their shoulders. Not improper. Not accidental. Precisely measured.

“I was not always in the habit of retreating,” he said at last. The remark did not sound like continuation of argument. It sounded, instead, like correction.

She did not look at him. “No?”

“My uncle,” he said, “had little patience for noise.” The word uncle landed with a faint weight. She had not known the detail, though she had inferred something of arrangement from his manner.

“He preferred order.”

The hedge curved inward, enclosing them more tightly.

“And did you provide it?” she asked.

“I learned to.” The answer was neither proud nor resentful. Simply stated.

She felt something in that settle against her own history with uncomfortable familiarity. There had been a season — she could still recall the exact sensation of it — when she had first understood that gratitude must be visible. That composure was expected. That affection, if offered too freely, might appear presumptuous. She had learned to sit straight. To speak when addressed. To anticipate correction before it was delivered. She had learned to regulate.

“And were you rewarded?” she asked.

He considered that briefly. “Yes. With stability.” Not warmth. He did not say it, but she heard the absence.

The enclosed garden opened slightly at its centre — a small fountain set within a circular bed, water falling in steady, disciplined measure. The sound was controlled, almost precise.

“Stability is not insignificant,” she said.

“No.” He glanced toward the fountain. “It permits movement,” he added, “within certain lines.”

She felt the truth of that more sharply than she expected. The Vale household had not been unkind. It had been generous in its way. Education. Protection. Position. But every gift had arrived with an invisible boundary. She had known, even at fourteen, that affection must not be assumed equal. That mistakes would weigh differently upon her. Stability had never quite belonged to her.

“You speak as though the lines were drawn before you arrived,” she said.

“In some houses,” he replied, “they are drawn before one is born.”

The fountain continued its steady fall.

She did not ask whether his uncle had drawn them. She did not ask whether he had ever stepped beyond them. Instead she said, “And do you still keep within them?”

He turned his head slightly, enough that she felt the movement rather than saw it. “I know where they are,” he said.

That was not an answer. She understood the distinction.

Ahead, two of the younger ladies had paused at the edge of the fountain, debating whether the water was cold enough to be disagreeable. Their laughter rang briefly against the stone.

Clara slowed her pace. It would be easy — dangerously easy — to continue this line of conversation. She had not intended to reveal even this much interest.

“You regulate,” he said again, more quietly now.

She did not deny it. “One must,” she replied. “In some circumstances.”

“And in others?”

She hesitated. In others, she had allowed everything. The memory flared — her own voice saying the word with more force than she had intended. She felt the heat return beneath her collar despite the shade. “In others,” she said evenly, “one must be certain.”

“Of what?”

“That the lines are not imaginary.”

The water fell, steady and unaltered.

He regarded her for a moment without speaking.

She was aware of the steadiness of his attention — not intrusive, not careless. Measured. That unsettled her more than open pursuit would have done.

She turned slightly toward the fountain, as though considering whether to join the others. It would not do to remain enclosed here much longer.

Footsteps sounded upon the gravel behind them — unhurried, unannounced. Clara knew the cadence before she turned.

Celestine moved along the path with effortless composure, the light catching cleanly along the pale fabric of her morning gown. She appeared neither surprised nor searching — as though the convergence had been incidental.

“Clara,” she said. The use of her name was quiet, uninflected. Familiar.

Clara stepped toward her at once. “Yes?”

“Mrs. Denham has asked whether you will read this afternoon.” Celestine’s tone was smooth, assured. “I have told her she may depend upon you.” It was not a question.

“Of course,” she said.

Celestine’s gaze rested on her for a moment longer, and she smiled faintly. “That will be most agreeable,” she said. As she spoke, her fingers adjusted the fall of Clara’s glove at the wrist — a small, neat correction, smoothing a crease Clara had not noticed. The touch was brief. Exact. The fabric lay flatter against her skin.

Beside her, she became aware that Mr. Harrow had gone very still. It was not a sound. It was the absence of one. The gravel had ceased shifting beneath his step.

Celestine inclined her head in his direction then, the gesture polite and sufficient. “Mr. Harrow.”

“Miss Vale.” His voice was level. If anything, more measured than before.

“Mrs. Denham anticipates a lively afternoon,” Celestine continued, addressing no one in particular. “We must not disappoint her.”

“Certainly not,” he said.

Clara sensed something taut in the air — a thin, tightened thread she could not name. She attributed it to proximity. To the narrowing of the path. To the enclosed garden.

Celestine’s hand rested lightly at Clara’s wrist for the briefest instant longer than necessary before falling away. “Do not wander too far,” she added. The remark might have applied to anyone. 

Clara nodded.

Celestine moved past them with the same composure with which she had arrived, joining the cluster near the fountain without pause. Within moments she was engaged in conversation with Mrs. Denham’s cousin, her expression attentive and serene.

The garden resumed its sound.

Clara became aware of her own pulse — steady, if a shade quicker than before. She turned back toward Mr. Harrow.

He had shifted his stance slightly. One hand rested at his back, fingers curled as though they had closed upon something that was no longer there. His jaw appeared more defined than it had moments before, though his expression remained perfectly civil. “You are much depended upon,” he said. There was nothing improper in the remark.

“No more than usual,” she replied.

“Is it usual,” he asked, “to be spoken for in such terms?” 

She wondered at the hint of sharpness in his tone. “It saves trouble,” she said. “Arrangements are best made in advance.”

“For whom?”

“For everyone.”

His gaze lingered briefly at her wrist — at the place Celestine had adjusted. She resisted the impulse to smooth it again.

“And you are content with that?” he asked.

“With being useful?”

“With being assigned.” The word struck her faintly, though she could not say why.

“I am accustomed to it,” she said.

He did not answer immediately.

The fountain continued its steady descent.

She felt, rather than saw, the restraint in him — as though something had tightened and then been deliberately held in check. “Miss Vale is fortunate,” he said at last, “to command such certainty.”

Clara inclined her head. “It is not command,” she replied. “It is expectation.”

He did not contradict her. But the air between them had altered.

Celestine’s voice drifted from the fountain — light, controlled, entirely at ease. Laughter answered it. The enclosed garden seemed smaller than it had moments before.

Clara became aware that Mr. Harrow had not resumed walking. Neither had she.

The others had drawn slightly ahead; the sound of water obscured distinct words.

“You are accustomed to it,” he repeated. There was no irony in his tone now.

“Yes.”

“To being expected.”

“It is not a hardship.”

“That was not my question.” The steadiness in him was sharper than before — not restless, not teasing. Focused. She felt it like a narrowing of space.

“You imply one,” she said.

He took a step nearer, not enough to breach propriety, but enough that she was conscious of the distance closing. “You speak of arrangement,” he said. “Of lines drawn in advance.”

“That is common enough.”

“For those expected to comply,” he replied. The word landed harder than he perhaps intended.

Her spine straightened. “I am not compelled.”

“I did not say you were.”

“You implied it.”

“I implied,” he said carefully, “that you are treated as such.”

The fountain’s steady fall seemed suddenly louder.

She felt heat rise again — not from embarrassment this time. “You presume much,” she said.

“I saw enough.”

Her pulse quickened. “You saw a courtesy.”

“I saw possession.” The word did not sound dramatic in his mouth. It sounded measured. Controlled.

Clara’s breath caught, though she could not have said why. “Miss Vale has always spoken for me in such matters,” she said. “It is simpler.”

“For whom?”

“For the household.”

“And for you?”

She hesitated. No one had ever asked her that so directly. “It prevents misunderstanding,” she said at last.

His gaze did not waver. “And what would occur,” he asked, “if you were misunderstood?”

The question lingered between them. She thought of fourteen. Of gratitude. Of correction delivered gently but unmistakably. Of how quickly error could alter atmosphere. “It is undesirable,” she said.

“Undesirable,” he repeated quietly. 

A step of silence passed between them.

The younger ladies at the fountain had begun to drift away, called back toward the larger path. The enclosed garden no longer offered the illusion of privacy.

“Do you belong so entirely elsewhere,” he said, “that you have no claim upon yourself?” The question did not feel rhetorical. It struck something low and unsettled.

Her throat constricted. “I belong where I am placed.” 

He did not smile. “And who placed you?” he asked.

The sound of gravel under approaching steps warned her that the others were returning.

She did not answer. Instead, she said, “You are mistaken if you imagine yourself in competition.”

“With whom?” The edge was sharper now.

“With circumstance.”

He held her gaze for a long moment. “I do not compete with circumstance,” he said. “I alter it.”

The words sent a fine tremor through her, and she felt her stomach realign itself.

Mrs. Denham’s voice carried nearer now, calling the group back toward the open lawn.

Clara turned toward the path. “You will find,” she said, more quietly than before, “it is not so easily altered.”

His expression did not soften. “We shall see.”

The breeze stirred again, lifting the leaves overhead.

Clara stepped forward before she could measure the wisdom of remaining where she stood.

## Chapter IV — The Game Proposed

The afternoon settled into a warmth too languid for industry. Chairs had been drawn into a loose semicircle upon the lawn; parasols tilted against the sun; Mrs. Denham declared that digestion required diversion of a gentler sort than walking. “Miss Ashbourne,” she said, with amiable insistence, “you will oblige us.”

Clara had known the request would come. The novel lay already upon the small table beside her chair — three modestly worn volumes of _The Mysteries of Udolpho_, their edges softened by circulation. She placed her fingers upon the page she had marked earlier. 

The garden was quieter than the drawing room had been. Sound dispersed into open air. There was nowhere for a voice to hide.

She began evenly. Radcliffe’s heroine spoke of distance — of a heart governed by prudence when inclination urged otherwise. Of gratitude owed. Of the discipline required to silence a preference that could not be declared. Clara read the sentences with proper cadence at first, careful not to overstep the boundaries of good taste. 

A murmur of approval followed an especially elegant turn of phrase. A gentleman shifted in his seat. Then the passage deepened.

_“She endeavoured to subdue a tenderness which honour forbade her to indulge; yet the more she commanded her feelings to be silent, the more eloquent they became within her own bosom.”_

Clara’s breath altered before she meant it to. She did not raise her voice. She lowered it instead — the words drawn inward, as though spoken less for the company than for the page itself.

_“It was not that she repented affection; she repented only the impossibility of avowing it.”_

The sentence settled into the air and did not disperse at once. She felt the familiar quickening — the same that had seized her the night before when she had said _everything_ without intending to. The edges of the lawn softened; the semicircle of chairs seemed less distinct. The heroine’s restraint did not feel ornamental. It felt exact. She allowed the next line its full weight.

_“Thus she learned to appear tranquil, while her heart was engaged in a conflict she dared not name.”_

The word tranquil lingered. Clara did not embellish it. She did not need to. When she reached the close of the passage, she did not immediately look up. The page remained open beneath her hand. 

The garden held its breath. A breeze passed through the upper leaves, but no one spoke. Clara became aware, slowly, of the stillness before her.

Mrs. Denham’s fan had ceased its movement. One of the younger ladies leaned forward as though to hear what had already been spoken. Even the distant murmur from the orchard had quieted.

She lifted her gaze.

Mr. Harrow sat with one hand resting loosely upon his knee, his attention fixed not upon the book, but upon her. There was no amusement in his expression. No quicksilver wit poised for reply. Only concentration. She felt the heat rise again beneath her collar. 

Her eyes shifted — almost without intention — toward Celestine. Miss Vale had not altered her posture. Her back remained perfectly straight; her gloved hands rested one upon the other in her lap. Yet her composure had stilled into something finer than politeness. The faint light upon her face had sharpened the blue of her eyes; they did not leave Clara. It was not scrutiny. It was not approval. It was something closer to absorption.

Clara’s pulse faltered. She lowered her gaze back to the page, though there was nothing left to read.

Mrs. Denham drew in a small breath. “How affecting,” she declared, as though recovering herself. “Miss Radcliffe never fails to agitate and console in equal measure.”

A gentleman murmured agreement. Chairs shifted. A parasol snapped closed and opened again. The air resumed its ordinary movement.

Clara closed the volume with care and placed it upon her lap. She felt, with sudden clarity, that something had moved — though nothing in the arrangement of chairs had changed. 

Celestine was the first to rise. “Miss Radcliffe has done her duty for the afternoon,” she said lightly. “We must not expect Miss Ashbourne to do more.” Her smile was easy. Perfectly composed. When she passed near Clara, her hand brushed briefly against the back of Clara’s chair. The contact was fleeting. Clara felt it as distinctly as if it had been deliberate.

Mr. Harrow stood a moment later. The warmth of the afternoon seemed sharper now.

Conversation resumed, but not smoothly. Mrs. Denham spoke of Radcliffe’s popularity; Mr. Fletcher protested that sensibility was too easily indulged; one of the younger ladies declared herself quite transported. The remarks hovered at the surface of the passage just read, as though none wished to descend again to the line where tranquillity had masked conflict.

Clara kept her hands folded over the closed volume. She had not intended to read it so. She had meant to maintain proper cadence — to preserve distance between text and voice. Instead, the words had pressed closer than they ought. She could still feel them along the back of her throat. 

She did not look for him. She was conscious of him nonetheless. Mr. Harrow disengaged from the curve of chairs and came toward her without haste. Not with the deliberate crossing of the drawing room the evening before — but with something quieter. “Miss Ashbourne,” he said.

She inclined her head.

“You have injured Radcliffe for me,” he added. The remark might have been playful. It was not delivered so.

“In what respect?” she asked.

“I shall not be able to read her again without hearing that passage in your voice.” Heat rose swiftly — sharper for the openness of the lawn.

“It is not mine,” she said. “It belongs to the page.”

“It did not,” he replied, “while you read it.” The simplicity of the statement unsettled her more than admiration would have done.

She felt the echo of the line again: _She learned to appear tranquil…_ “You mistake performance for possession,” she said, though the words felt thin.

“I mistake nothing,” he said. There was no levity in it.

Around them, the semicircle of chairs had loosened. Two gentlemen debated whether Emily’s sensibility was excessive; Mrs. Denham fanned herself with renewed animation.

“You spoke,” he continued, lowering his voice by a degree, “as though the conflict were not confined to fiction.” 

Her pulse faltered. “That is the purpose of fiction,” she replied. “To resemble experience.”

“And does it?”

She should have answered lightly. She should have diverted. Instead, she said, “Often.” The word lay exposed between them.

He regarded her — not boldly, not invasively. Simply steadily.

Clara became acutely aware that she had not yet fully resumed her composure. The reading had left something unguarded. She drew her fingers together more tightly.

“It is dangerous,” he said quietly, “to allow so much.” The remark did not accuse. It acknowledged.

She lifted her chin slightly. “Dangerous for whom?”

“For anyone who mistakes restraint for indifference.” The words struck with unexpected force.

She did not answer at once.

Across the lawn, Celestine disengaged from Mrs. Denham with a smile that appeared effortless. The motion was unhurried. Controlled. Clara felt the shift before she turned her head. Miss Vale moved toward the centre of the lawn, the afternoon light catching at the pale fabric of her gown. Her expression was composed — perhaps more composed than before. The conversation faltered again, as though awaiting her direction. Clara sensed faintly that something in the air required redirection.

Mr. Harrow’s attention did not waver. 

“You need not persuade me of the virtues of restraint,” she said at last.

“I had no such intention.”

“What then?”

He did not smile. “Merely to observe that it is not always what it appears.” The remark hovered between them.

Celestine’s voice rose lightly. “We are in danger,” she said, “of dissolving entirely into reflection.” 

Several of the guests laughed — too readily, perhaps, but with gratitude for the release. Mrs. Denham declared that reflection was ill-suited to such weather. A gentleman proposed whist; another objected that cards in sunlight were an offence to nature.

Celestine stood at the centre of the loose circle, her composure restored to its customary ease. “We have been solemn long enough,” she said. “Miss Radcliffe must forgive us. I propose something less improving.”

“A walk?” Mr. Fletcher ventured.

“We have walked.”

“Then what, Miss Vale?” one of the younger ladies asked eagerly.

Celestine’s gaze moved across the assembly — not searching, not selecting, merely including. “A game,” she said.

The word stirred immediate animation. Chairs scraped softly against the grass. Fans lowered. A ripple of anticipation travelled through the company.

“What sort of game?” Mrs. Denham demanded.

“One that rewards observation,” Celestine replied, the faintest suggestion of amusement at the corner of her mouth. “Since we have so many who value it.”

There was laughter.

“Hide-and-seek!” cried one of the younger ladies, half daring the suggestion.

Celestine did not hesitate. “Precisely.”

The enthusiasm that followed was genuine. The house, with its staircases and alcoves and long corridors, presented itself at once as territory. Gentlemen straightened. Ladies exchanged glances.

Ribbons were fetched; pale lengths of satin drawn from a sewing basket that had been left near the veranda steps. Celestine accepted them as though she had expected their availability. “We shall pair off,” she said lightly. “Bound at the wrist. It prevents desertion.” The remark was met with delighted protest.

“And the seekers?” asked Mr. Fletcher.

“Unbound,” she replied. “No one leaves the house. The parlour shall serve as our beginning and our end.” The rules fell into place without resistance.

Clara rose more slowly than the others. The reading had left her unsettled; the proposal of play did not disperse that feeling so much as redirect it. Movement hummed through the lawn — a brightening energy that made stillness conspicuous.

Mr. Harrow stepped toward her again. “You cannot mean to abandon the field,” he said quietly. The lightness of the tone did not conceal its intent.

“I have made no declaration,” she replied.

“Then make one.”

She felt the residual heat from the reading rise again; not embarrassment now, but something closer to exhilaration. The notion of being bound, wrist to wrist, in concealment struck her with unreasonable clarity.

“And if I decline?” she asked.

“I should be compelled to search without object,” he said. “Which would render the exercise intolerable.” The admission was unadorned. She did not mistake it.

Her composure wavered by a fraction. “You presume preference,” she said.

“I state it.” The directness unsettled her — not unpleasantly.

Around them, pairs had begun to form. Laughter flared and subsided as ribbons were compared and measured.

He reached toward the small heap of satin. “Shall we?” The word held no jest.

For a moment — brief but real — Clara imagined the ribbon binding them together. The enforced proximity. The impossibility of retreat. She smiled before she had entirely regulated the impulse.

His expression warmed. He took up a length of ribbon. “Then—”

“Mr. Harrow.” Celestine’s voice entered between them, smooth, almost amused. She stood only a few paces away, a ribbon already looped loosely between her fingers. “You must partner me,” she said. “I refuse to trust my escape to anyone less observant.” Her tone was kind. Light. There was no reproach in it.

Clara felt the shift as something clean and swift. Harrow paused — a fraction of stillness — before inclining his head. “Miss Vale.”

Celestine stepped forward and extended her wrist. The satin lay pale against her glove. 

Clara’s hands remained empty at her sides.

The afternoon seemed suddenly too bright.

Celestine did not hurry. She stepped close enough that the pale ribbon between her fingers brushed lightly against the cuff of Mr. Harrow’s coat. The satin caught the sun and flashed faintly. “You would not abandon me to incompetence,” she said, smiling as though the matter were self-evident. There was no tension in her tone. No challenge. Only certainty.

Harrow inclined his head again, more fully this time. “I should not dare.”

Clara stood where she was. She had not moved toward the ribbons. She had not stepped back. She simply remained; the moment suspended around her without invitation.

Celestine extended her wrist. 

For a breath, nothing happened.

Then Harrow took the ribbon from her hand. His fingers were precise. Controlled. He wound the satin once about her wrist, then his own. The motion was efficient, almost formal — but the act itself was intimate in its simplicity. When he drew the knot firm, their hands shifted closer than politeness would otherwise require.

Celestine’s gloved fingers brushed the inside of his wrist as she adjusted the lay of the ribbon. The contact might have been incidental. 

Clara felt the small tightening in her chest before she could prevent it.

Celestine looked up. Not at him. At Clara. The glance was brief — open, almost warm. “We shall depend upon your discretion,” she said lightly. “You must not betray us.” The words were playful. The tone invited complicity.

Clara managed a small smile. “I should not think of it.”

The ribbon lay smooth and pale between their wrists.

Around them, the lawn had erupted into delighted disorder. Other pairs fumbled with knots and laughed at their own clumsiness. One gentleman protested that the arrangement placed him at a disadvantage; his partner declared it a just punishment.

Mrs. Denham clapped her hands once in encouragement.

“You must not delay. The seekers grow impatient.”

Clara did not move toward the remaining ribbons. A younger gentleman glanced in her direction. “Miss Ashbourne, you still require a partner?”

“I believe,” she said, steadying her voice, “that I shall sit this out.” The words fell lightly enough.

“No!” protested one of the younger ladies. “You must not.”

“I insist,” Clara replied, smiling as though the refusal were of no consequence. “Someone must receive the triumphant.”

Mrs. Denham accepted the reasoning at once. “Very sensible.”

Celestine inclined her head, acknowledging the decision without comment. 

Harrow’s gaze met Clara’s at last. There was no jest in it now.

The ribbon drew him subtly toward Celestine as she shifted her weight, testing the length between them.

“We must not be the first discovered,” she said.

He did not answer.

The house doors stood open behind them, shadowed and cool. Pairs began moving toward the veranda steps, ribbons catching the light as they went. Laughter spilled ahead of them.

Clara remained upon the lawn.

Celestine and Harrow moved together toward the entrance — bound, aligned. Just before crossing the threshold, he turned once more. Only a glance. Then the house swallowed them.

## Chapter V — The Parlour

The last flash of pale ribbon vanished beyond the threshold; the doors were drawn shut with more enthusiasm than care. Laughter spilled once into the hall, then fractured upward along the staircase.

“One must count properly!” a gentleman’s voice called from within.

“Fifty!” another protested.

“Not before we are settled!”

The seekers’ counting began anyway — loud, theatrical, deliberately slow. The sound echoed faintly against the high ceiling of the entry.

Clara remained where she stood on the lawn for half a second longer than necessary. The open doorway now showed only shadowed floorboards and the faint gleam of the banister. The brightness of the afternoon pressed against her back. Then Mrs. Denham clapped once in encouragement, and the older ladies turned toward the house with mild interest. 

“We may as well observe the chaos from comfort,” Mrs. Fletcher declared, with mild approval rather than censure. 

Clara inclined her head and followed them inside. The parlour had been left in a state of loose abandonment — chairs not quite restored after the reading, a ribbon dropped upon the side table, one parasol forgotten near the window. The air inside was cooler, the scent of the afternoon filtered by walls and wood. From the hall came the final numbers of the count.

“…forty-eight—”

A suppressed shriek from somewhere above.

“—forty-nine—”

Footsteps thundered across the upper landing, then split — some toward the west wing, others toward the servants’ corridor. A door banged. Laughter ricocheted against the stairwell.

“—fifty!”

A cheer followed, then silence — the charged silence before pursuit.

Mrs. Fletcher lowered herself into a chair with deliberate composure. “Youth,” she observed, folding her hands neatly in her lap. “It must expend itself somewhere.”

Mrs. Denham settled opposite her, fanning gently. “If they overturn nothing of value, I shall count it a success.”

Clara chose a seat near the centre of the room, neither too near the doorway nor entirely removed from it. The book she had read from lay where she had left it. She did not touch it. Her hands folded in her lap without instruction.

Above them, the hunt began. Footsteps — quick, uneven — crossed the floorboards overhead. A door creaked open, hesitated, closed again. A muffled laugh broke off abruptly, as though stifled against fabric. The sound of running returned, closer to the stairhead, then veered away.

Clara kept her gaze fixed upon the mantel. 

She could almost trace their movements from the rhythm alone. The west corridor — narrower, echoing more sharply. The long gallery — steps carrying farther before dissolving. A door opening softly, carefully.

Ribbon drawn taut. The image came without invitation. She saw it — not clearly, but with sufficient detail to unsettle her. Celestine turning sharply into a side passage; Harrow following half a step behind; the satin between their wrists pulling them momentarily closer than intended. The brush of sleeve against sleeve. A breath nearly shared.

Clara adjusted her fingers against one another, pressing her thumb hard into her knuckle.

A door slammed somewhere above. A gentleman’s triumphant shout followed, then the answering peal of a young lady’s laughter — bright, breathless, unguarded.

“Already?” Mrs. Denham murmured with indulgent surprise.

“Impatient seekers are the ruin of such games,” Mrs. Fletcher replied mildly.

Footsteps pounded across the landing toward the staircase. Clara felt herself lean forward before she meant to, listening for the rhythm — heavy? light? single? paired? The steps slowed near the top of the stairs. An indistinct voice protested something in mock outrage. Then two sets of feet descended in uneven coordination, the faint slide of satin against fabric marking each shared movement. Clara’s pulse shifted. But the voices were wrong; too high, too careless.

The pair burst into the hall in laughter, nearly colliding with the side table before catching themselves. The ribbon between their wrists had been wound hastily; it tugged at each step.

“Linen press!” the gentleman declared before anyone had asked. “I assure you it was entirely accidental.”

“Accidental?” his partner gasped. “You closed the door upon my sleeve!”

“And saved us from discovery for at least twenty seconds,” he replied with pride.

They entered the parlour still laughing, flushed and disordered, cheeks bright with exertion. The ribbon was untied in full view, fingers fumbling at the knot.

Clara watched the pale skin at the young lady’s wrist — a faint red impression where the satin had pressed. The mark was not severe. It would fade within minutes.

A linen press. Darkness. Narrow shelves. The scent of starch and folded sheets. Two bodies forced close for lack of space rather than choice. The door closed. Breath contained. “For disorder,” she said.

Harrow — closer than propriety would dictate, because propriety was not present in an empty chamber. The ribbon slack. He lifts his hand to straighten it. His fingers brush her glove. A pause. Spoken as if testing its weight between them. 

“Clara.”

Her pulse hammered. She imagined not retreating. Imagined lifting her face instead of lowering it. Imagined the satin tightening when she did so. His mouth near enough that breath mingled before lips did. The moment before surrender. The suspended second in which decision could still reverse itself. She felt it — that suspended edge — as sharply as if it were happening against her own skin.

“Miss Ashbourne?”

Her eyes opened at once. 

Mrs. Fletcher regarded her with mild concern. “You are quite flushed.”

The chamber dissolved. The panelling vanished. The ribbon slackened into nothing. “I am well,” Clara said, though her voice required more steadiness than she preferred.

Above them — A sound. A low exhale that might have been breath. Might have been floorboards settling. Then — A single footstep. Near the stair. Another. Descending. This time the rhythm did not turn back.

Clara made an effort not to rise. She kept her hands folded, though her pulse struck hard against the inside of her wrist, as if the imagined ribbon had tightened again.

Before they appeared, another pair tumbled down the staircase in laughter — breathless, triumphant, nearly colliding with one another in their haste.

“Caught at last!” the gentleman announced.

“You stumbled over a stool,” his partner accused.

They entered the parlour flushed and delighted, their ribbon half-undone from struggling fingers. Mrs. Denham clapped lightly. Mrs. Fletcher smiled. Clara barely saw them.

The staircase creaked again. Slower. 

Celestine appeared first. For the briefest second — before she crossed the threshold fully into the light — Clara saw something unguarded. A stillness that did not belong to play. Her mouth was set slightly differently. Her gaze unfocused by a fraction, as though she had not yet re-entered the room she was stepping into. Then — almost visibly — she resumed herself. Her shoulders eased. Her expression smoothed. The faint curve returned to her lips. By the time she reached the doorway, serenity had settled over her like a well-fitted shawl.

The ribbon between her wrist and Mr. Harrow’s remained intact. Harrow followed half a pace behind. His coat was neat. His hair undisturbed. There was nothing outwardly altered in him — and yet everything in him felt more defined. The earlier playfulness had burned away. What remained was intent.

“We had nearly declared you victorious,” Mrs. Denham called pleasantly.

Celestine inclined her head. “We were not so fortunate,” she replied. Her voice was even — but Clara heard the faint delay before the word fortunate. As though the choice of it required care.

“Where were you concealed?” one of the younger gentlemen demanded.

“In the east corridor,” she said lightly. “It rewards patience.” Her gaze flickered — not across the room at large, but briefly, precisely — toward Clara. It did not linger.

The ribbon lay slightly twisted between their wrists. Celestine lifted her free hand and began to loosen the knot. Her fingers were steady. Only someone watching closely would notice that she drew the satin away more slowly than necessary — as though reluctant to let it fall. When the ribbon slackened, it brushed across the inside of Harrow’s wrist before slipping free.

He did not move to help her. He did not look at her. He was already looking at Clara. The shift in his gaze was immediate. Direct. Unshielded.

Celestine finished unwinding the ribbon and let it fall into her palm. For a fraction of a second, she stood still, the pale satin gathered loosely in her hand, her expression almost distant again. Then she folded it once. Twice. Neatly.

“A spirited diversion,” she said, and this time the composure held without effort. Laughter answered her. Clara felt something constrict in her chest. 

Harrow stepped fully into the parlour now that he was unbound. He did not join the laughter. He took one step toward Clara. The movement was small. No one remarked upon it. But the air between them tightened as distinctly as if satin had drawn them together again.

Clara half-rose out of her seat before she knew what was happening, and then stood up as if she had always meant to. He stopped before her.

“Miss Ashbourne,” he said. His voice was lower than before the game. It betrayed no playfulness, just sharp focus. And something in it carried the echo of what had not happened upstairs — or had.

## Chapter VI — The Proposal

“Miss Ashbourne.” His voice did not rise above the hum of the room, yet it seemed to draw the sound inward rather than compete with it.

“Yes?” she answered. She had meant to speak evenly. The word emerged more quietly than intended.

Behind him, Mrs. Denham was recounting the ingenuity of one of the younger gentlemen who had concealed himself behind the pianoforte. Laughter flared and receded. Mrs. Fletcher asked whether refreshments ought to be restored to the table. The room continued.

Harrow did not. “Are you leaving in the morning?” he asked. The directness of it struck cleanly.

Clara’s spine stiffened by a degree she hoped was not visible. “It has been discussed,” she replied. “Nothing is fixed.”

“She told me it was.” The pronoun required no clarification. 

Clara’s gaze flickered, despite herself, toward Celestine. Miss Vale stood near the window now, the folded ribbon resting against her palm. She was listening to Mr. Fletcher’s account of the west gallery, her expression attentive and serene. If she had heard the question, she did not betray it.

“Told you,” Clara repeated.

“That you leave in the morning.” His eyes did not soften. “That Hartwell Grove is a pleasant interruption.” The words carried the faintest echo of Celestine’s cadence — composed, almost philosophical.

Clara felt heat rise along her collarbone. “It may be,” she said. “We do not linger where we are not required.”

“And are you not required?” The sharpness was slight — but deliberate.

She steadied herself. “I have not been dismissed.”

“That was not my meaning.”

“What was?” she asked.

“That you speak of departure as though it were weather.” His voice remained low. Controlled. “As though it arrives whether you consent to it or not.” The accusation — if it was one — landed closer than she wished.

“It often does,” she said. 

Behind him, a gentleman called for wine. Someone proposed another round of the game. The air was warm again with chatter.

Harrow took half a step nearer — not enough to breach propriety, but enough that she felt the shift in distance. “Did you tell her you would go?” he asked.

“I knew we might.” 

“That was not the question.”

Clara lifted her chin. “You inquire as though it concerns you.”

“It does.” The simplicity of it disordered her more than any ornamented declaration might have done.

She became acutely aware of the space between them — narrow enough now that she could see the faint pulse at his throat, the steadiness in his gaze. No playfulness remained. No feigned ease.

“I do not presume,” he replied. “I refuse.”

“Refuse what?”

“To have you removed by courtesy.” The word struck her — not violently, but precisely. Removed.

Across the room, Celestine’s laugh sounded — light, measured. It did not waver.

Clara forced her breath to steady. “I am not an object to be removed.”

“No,” he said. “You are allowing yourself to be.” The room seemed suddenly too bright.

“I belong where I am placed,” she said, hearing the repetition even as she spoke it.

His jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. “And who placed you?” The question lingered — not loud enough to draw attention, yet sharp enough to cut through the hum of conversation.

Her voice faltered. 

“Did you tell her you would go?” he asked again.

“It was not settled.”

“It is now.”

“She may have spoken with certainty,” Clara said, choosing her words carefully. “She values clarity.”

“Clarity,” he repeated, as though testing it. “Or conclusion?” The word landed between them — hers, returned.

From the window, Celestine shifted her weight slightly, her gaze moving — not abruptly, not searching — but grazing the room until it reached them. For the briefest instant her eyes met Clara’s. “Here?” she asked.

“Now.” The insistence unsettled her more than raised voices would have done. 

Around them, chairs scraped; glasses were passed; Mrs. Denham was recounting the merits of spirited youth.

“It is unnecessary,” she said quietly.

“Clara.”

Celestine’s voice drifted across the room. “We must not exhaust ourselves before dinner,” she was saying, serene as ever. She did not look at them again.

Clara drew a slow breath. “Very well,” she said.

He did not offer his arm. He turned, trusting she would follow.

And she did.

The noise of the parlour dulled behind them as they turned into the corridor. The air was cooler here, thinner, carrying only the faint echo of laughter through plaster and wood. Their footsteps sounded too clear against the polished floor. 

He did not stop immediately. He walked as though he meant to outrun the room itself, and she followed because to hesitate would have been visible. Only when they reached the bend near the staircase did he turn.

“Yes.”

## Chapter VII — Combustion

His bedroom door had closed without either of them remembering who pushed it. She was still kissing him. ## Chapter VIII — Carriage

Morning arrives without ceremony.

The darkness loosens first at the edges of the room, thinning into a pale grey that gathers along the curtains and creeps across the carved foot of the bed. Clara becomes aware of it before she opens her eyes — the coolness of air where warmth had pressed close all night.

She lies still. The memory of him lingers in her body — a slow ache, not unpleasant. A warmth that has not yet entirely receded.

His smile curves faintly, reassuring. “I am only certain that you are not obliged to go.” The phrasing is careful.
