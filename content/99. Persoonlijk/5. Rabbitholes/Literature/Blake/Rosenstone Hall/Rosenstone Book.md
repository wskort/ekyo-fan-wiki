# Rosenstone Hall
## Chapter One

**Arrival**

They arrived at Longbourn Park two days before the ball, when the house was still in the midst of becoming itself.

Servants moved through the corridors in purposeful disorder, bearing chairs that would later be arranged into symmetrical obedience, flowers still bound in damp cloth, trays that would be polished and repolished until no trace of handling remained. Doors stood ajar where they would soon be shut; voices carried where music would later swallow them whole.

Blake Earnest observed all of it without appearing to look.

She stepped from the carriage after Duchess Andie Springs, waited precisely long enough to be seen waiting, and then followed. Her hands were already folded. Her shawl lay straight upon her shoulders. She neither gazed about with the curiosity of a guest nor shrank with the unease of one who feared she did not belong. She walked as though she had been here before — not because she had, but because it was safer to behave as if one had.

“Mind the gravel, Blake,” the duchess said lightly, without turning.

Blake adjusted her step at once.  
“Yes, Your Grace.”

The entrance hall rose before them in pale stone and measured grandeur. Blake noted the placement of doors first: one to the left that would lead to the servants’ passage, another half-concealed behind a column that likely connected to the back stairs. She registered the distance between the foot of the staircase and the nearest window, the fall of light at this hour of the afternoon, the small table set too near the wall to be purely ornamental.

Routes. Thresholds. Spaces that were not meant to linger.

Lady Mal de Courcy herself was not present to receive them — a circumstance that did not trouble Duchess Andie Springs in the slightest. The housekeeper appeared instead, a woman of composed authority and efficient manner, offering apologies with one hand already gesturing them onward.

“Her ladyship regrets that she is detained,” the housekeeper said. “If Your Grace will permit me, I shall see you settled.”

Blake stepped forward at once to take the duchess’s cloak, sparing the housekeeper the effort.

“You are quite invaluable, Blake,” the duchess remarked absently, her attention already upon the hall.

Blake inclined her head.  
“Thank you, Your Grace.”

They were shown into the east drawing room, where the windows stood open to the afternoon air and a small company of ladies was already assembled: two in quiet conversation near the window, another seated with a book she did not appear to be reading.

The housekeeper paused, fulfilling the last obligation of civility.

“Your Grace,” she said, “may I present Miss Kamryn Emerald.”

Miss Emerald rose immediately.

“This is Duchess Andie Springs,” the housekeeper continued, “and Miss Blake Earnest.”

“Miss Emerald,” the duchess said, with a gracious inclination of her head that required no return.

“Your Grace,” Miss Emerald replied.

Blake inclined her head.  
“Miss Emerald.”

“Miss Earnest,” Miss Emerald said in return, her tone warm but properly restrained.

The housekeeper waited just long enough to see the greetings concluded, then inclined her head to the duchess.

“If there is anything Your Grace requires, I shall be just outside,” she said, and withdrew, closing the door softly behind her.

The duchess, having thus concluded the matter of introductions, moved directly to the chaise nearest the mirror, evidently having already decided that it was hers.

Blake remained standing just long enough to ensure no further instruction was forthcoming, then set herself to usefulness: removing gloves, aligning a side table that had been nudged from its place, setting aside a tray of untouched teacups.

The duchess lowered herself onto the chaise and extended her legs slightly, studying her reflection with interest.

“I shall want you nearby this evening,” she said. “Lady Mal is particular.”

“Of course, Your Grace.”

“And while we have a moment,” Duchess Andie Springs continued, reaching for a bell she did not ring, “you might as well practise your writing. Your penmanship would benefit from steadiness.”

Blake paused only long enough to acknowledge the instruction.  
“Very well, Your Grace.”

“There is paper upon the escritoire,” the duchess said, already losing interest. “One never knows when one may be required to write.”

Blake crossed to the writing desk set near the window. The paper laid out there was thick and faintly cream-coloured, the sort reserved for correspondence of consequence. She set the inkwell carefully to one side and took up the pen.

And stopped.

For a moment, nothing happened.

There was no visible distress, no sign of embarrassment. Only stillness — the pen hovering just above the page.

Outside, a cart rattled along the drive. Somewhere down the corridor, a door closed with practised quiet.

“I thought that might be awkward.”

Miss Emerald’s voice came from behind her — not abrupt, merely present.

Blake turned her head slightly.  
“It is manageable, Miss Emerald.”

“I do not doubt that, Miss Earnest,” Kamryn said, stepping nearer. “But still.”

Her gaze moved from the blank page to Blake’s face.  
“If you wish, you might write to no one in particular. A gentleman of your own imagining. It would satisfy the exercise without obligating you to anything.”

Blake considered this.  
“I should not wish to misrepresent myself.”

Miss Emerald smiled, not unkindly.  
“My dear Miss Earnest, that is precisely what practice allows — misrepresentation without consequence. You need not send it. Think of it as rehearsal.”

She lowered her voice slightly.  
“You may even leave the name blank.”

Blake returned her attention to the page.

After a moment, she began to write.

The words came carefully, without haste or ornament. She wrote of reading, of walking, of keeping a household in good order. She wrote nothing of longing, nothing of ambition. When she reached the end, she signed her name with a steadiness that sat in quiet contrast to the modesty of the lines above it.

Miss Emerald read over her shoulder, her expression altering as she went.

When she reached the end, she did not speak at once.

“That is,” she said at last, “very honest.”

“I intended it to be.”

“Yes.” Miss Emerald straightened, thoughtful. “Yes, I see that.”

She hesitated, then shook her head slightly.  
“I had considered whether it might be delivered somewhere — but no. Not as it stands.”

Blake received this without comment.

“Not because it lacks anything,” Miss Emerald added quickly, and with sincerity. “But because it would be read unkindly.”

“I understand,” Blake said.

And she did.

Miss Emerald gathered the pages together and returned them neatly to the desk.  
“Practice is practice,” she said. “There is no harm in leaving it at that.”

At that moment, Duchess Andie Springs rose from the chaise and cast a brief glance toward the escritoire. Miss Emerald stepped back at once.

“Well?” the duchess asked.

“It is done, Your Grace,” Blake replied.

“Very good.” The duchess smoothed her skirts. “Come along, then. I should like to inspect the gardens before the light fades.”

Blake capped the ink, aligned the paper once more, and rose.

Outside, Longbourn Park continued its transformation — becoming orderly, becoming beautiful, becoming ready.

Blake followed the duchess from the room, leaving the desk precisely as she had found it.

There were other preparations to be made.

*She is young enough that no one thinks to explain.*

*The place is public — a broad step near the edge of a square, where people pass without looking closely. She is told to sit and wait. The instruction is given without emphasis, as though it requires none.*

*The parcel is wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. It rests in her lap. It smells faintly of food — bread, perhaps, or some kind of pastry.* 

*She is hungry.*

*But she knows the paper would show it if she disturbed it. A crease out of place, a tear at the corner.*

*She sits.*

*People pass. Shoes scuff the ground. Someone laughs. A cart rattles by. No one pays her any mind. She does not adjust her grip. She does not shift the parcel to peer beneath the fold. She does not lick her fingers.*

*Time stretches, but not unbearably. Hunger is not new. It is not urgent. It is simply present.*

*At some point she thinks she might have been forgotten. Would they come back? Would they mind if she took just a peek? Would they get angry?*

*She remains where she is.*

*When the person returns, they do not hurry. They take the parcel from her hands and glance at it once, briefly, as if checking that it is as it ought to be.*

*“Good,” they say. “You’re still here.”*

*They turn and go.*

*She stays seated for a moment longer, because no one has yet told her otherwise.*

*Then she stands and follows.*
